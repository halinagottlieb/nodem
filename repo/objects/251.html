Theatrical Masquerade Narrative - A proposal for the Verdi Museum
Date added:  2013-12-31 13:20:58
Modified:        2013-12-31 13:24:26
Author / Creator Lily Diazhttp://mlab.uiah.fi/people/sho...
Author / Creator Mika Tuomola
Museum/site: Verdi Museum, Italy Country: Finland Developers: Lily Diaz, Mika Tuomola Sponsor partnerships: Submitted by: University of Art and Design Helsinki, Media Lab Project website: -- Project status: idea Project type: Large scale/Immersive Installation Start year: 2003 End year: 2003 Summary: Our design approach handles the museum space as theatrical spectacle, “Opera Verdi.” This would suggest creating a setting, in which museum visitors, almost without noticing it, become participants in the spectacle, actors that by their movement and interactions with props-as-interfaces strengthen the existence of the spectacle to other visitors as well. Creating such a setting requires the use of the principles and personnel (writer, composer, actors, director and production crew) of dramatic discipline in addition to the museum exhibit design and researcher team. Intentions Who am I? A Portrait of Verdi The theatrical masquerade can already be started by the museum entrance/exit, where the visitor will find the talking Portrait of Verdi. The two-sided portrait shows its other face to those who enter the exhibit, and the other face to those who are exiting. As a matter fact, it’s constructed out of two high-resolution flat-screens, beautifully framed, and two hidden, directed microphones that detect visitors voices, when they look at the portrait’s opposite sides. The portrait’s entrance side becomes lip-sync animated and gets its (manipulated) voice from the exit side microphone, so the visitors exiting the museum can give their voice to the Verdi portrait welcoming people. At the same time, as the entering people can do the same to the exiting people, they can involve in conversation with “Verdi” about Verdi (or any other matter, as a matter of fact). The primary purpose of the masquerade is to set the visitors in mood for playful interaction and for adopting various roles during the museum tour. However, it may also function as an important socialising element between the entering and exiting museum visitors, who may use it for really remarkable changes of bits of personally important knowledge and feelings about Verdi, music, the exhibit etcetera – the kind of bits that provide the exhibit information with a point-of-view and thus with an important engaging element in narrative and drama. Ultimately, it can also be used to help to choreograph and orchestrate the movement of individuals and groups through the museum. The Props, the Movement The concept of props-as-interface is related to the power of objects to bring forth a progression or passage in a narrative. The stories of humankind are, after all, embedded in the objects it produces. Utilised cleverly, props-as-interfaces can help us to integrate many of the technological elements throughout the museum. At the same time, they can be used to separate the many interpretations – or points of view – possible from the data presented. In the Verdi Museum, objects of material culture can be employed to make the technological aspects of the interface more transparent (or invisible) to the guests in the exhibition. A pair of specially designed opera glasses, for example, can provide an elegant solution to the problem of transmission of sound data, location coordinates, as well as serve as trigger device for several ‘magical’ displays in the exhibition. At the same time, the choice of glasses might afford the visitor with a particular point of view, or interpretation of the general exhibition. The feasibility of the concept was investigated as part of the Displaying Culture, research project and demonstrated in the Semana Santa in Carúpano, Venezuela exhibition held at the Helinä Rautavaara Museum (1999-2000). In this exhibition, props were extensively used to involve the viewer in the narrative of the exhibition. The Characters, the Relationship The various points-of-view provided by the props-as-interface are made strongly engaging only by interesting characters, with which the visitor can identify or form a relationship with. For example, Verdi’s three wives may be the ones telling their points-of-views about the man. Imagine that there were three kinds of opera glasses, each type belonging to a different woman – to Margherita, Peppina, or Teresa. Depending on the opera glasses you would hear only one of them speaking about Verdi, different stories depending on your location at the museum, on what you are looking at (this is made possible by a tracking system and centrally transmitted audio-feedback in the theatre glasses). Imagine that there were a couple of places where their ghosts still existed in the museum and could appear visually to the visitor (centrally projected from a video-clip database using fluent algorithmic editing depending on the sound one was listening). In order to increase the style/genre of various tours to suit various visitors’ most keen interests and to expand their existing interest to other aspects of Verdi, the three significant ladies could be dramatised and contemporised, for example, into the classic triangle of the three Fates, the Fates of Verdi: they represent not only the past, contemporary and future influence of the composer, but also the various aspects of his life from tragic to comic and beyond – to the (musical and other) influences we may not even be able to tie to the man, or any man, individually. The dramatic and technical feasibility of the concept of using real-time edited moving image and sound in creating believable interactive characters has been tested, for example, in the hypermovies Vala (dir. Mauren Thomas, Runecast, Cambridge University Moving Image Studio & Interactive Institute 2001) and Myths for One (dir. Mika Tuomola, Media Lab & Lume, University of Art and Design Helsinki 2002), and is supported by Leena Saarinen’s research Chatterbots: Crash Test Dummies of Communication (University of Art and Design Helsinki 2001). Mika Tuomola and Leena Saarinen are also continuing the development of the usability of such interactive characters in various environments in their on-going production Accidental Lovers and in virtual worlds. The operatic rhetoric and character expression research for the “ghosts” can start, for example, from the expression of the old, venerable women of Casa Verdi, the Milanese retirement home for opera stars founded in 1902 by Verdi and originally supported by his royalties, as described in the wonderful 1985 documentary by Daniel Schmid. Tech spec: Comments: We introduce here a preliminary vision of the design approach for the Verdi Museum in Italy.
