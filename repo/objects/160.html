Mechanics of Wonder: Scripting visitor experiences with technology
Date added:  2013-10-16 11:10:52
Modified:        2014-01-15 18:32:30
Author / Creator Kevin Walkerhttp://ied.rca.ac.uk
Author / Creator Suzy Hogghttp://suz.co.uk
Author / Creator David di Ducahttp://diduca.co.uk
We describe an experiment using digital technology to influence visitors' movements, motivations and meaning making. The exhibition Panopticon: Experimental Tales of Jeremy Bentham explored the life, influence and radical ideas of the 18th Century philosopher, best known for the prison he designed, the Panopticon. The exhibition ran during August and September 2013 at UCL in London. We augmented historical documents and images with digital projections to create multiple narrative sequences using lighting and interpretive text, in order to influence visitor meaning making and engineer a sense of wonder using narrative storytelling structures. This follows from research on visitor-generated trails (Walker, 2013), but inverts the principle by imposing particular trails in a top-down curatorial fashion. Visitor movements were tracked using a 3D depth camera to provide quantitative data, and meaning making was evaluated using Personal Meaning Making, an established method for generating qualitative data (Falk, 2003). As an experimental setting, the exhibition was held in a small space and the number of exhibited objects was limited to a small number in order to be retained in visitors' short-term memory, following Miller (1956); access to the exhibition was restricted to individual and small groups of visitors at a time, each of which were timed. The data is to be analysed by comparing the qualitative with quantitative data to evaluate the extent to which each visitor followed the curated narrative, and the extent to which their knowledge and understanding changed as a result. Motivation is analysed using the model of Csikszentmihalyi and Hermanson (1999). We will present our results for the first time at NODEM 2013. References Csikszentmihalyi, M. & Hermanson, K. (1999). Intrinsic Motivation in Museums: Why Does One Want to Learn? In Hooper-Greenhill, E. (Ed.) The Educational Role of the Museum, 146-160. Falk, J. H. (2003). Personal meaning mapping. In G. Caban, et al (Eds.), Museums and creativity: A study into the role of museums in design education. Sydney: Powerhouse Publishing. Miller, G.A. (1956) The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information. Psychological Review, 63, 81-97. Walker, K. (2013) Designing for museum learning. In Luckin, R., et al (Eds.) Routledge Handbook on Design in Educational Computing.

    Kevin Walker

    Kevin Walker is a researcher, designer, writer and artist working at the boundaries of digital and physical – specifically in curation and computation in physical spaces, grounded in cognitive and cultural theory. Author of Hackers and Slackers (2012), co-editor of Digital Technologies and the Museum Experience (2008), his background is in journalism, design, interactive media and education research. Kevin Walker studied journalism and media, before moving into advertising and graphic design in the 1980s. He obtained a BA in Anthropology and Mass Communications from the University of California, Berkeley in 1992, before working in radio for a while. He then gained a Masters in Interactive Telecommunication from New York University, focusing on physical computing, applying this subsequently as Senior Software Designer for Exhibitions at the American Museum of Natural History in New York in the late 1990's. Since then Kevin has continued to design museum exhibits and media as well as work with artists to help them realise their technical ambitions. Kevin moved to London in 2003 to pursue doctoral research in Museums and Technology, completing this in 2010. Simultaneously he participated in funded research projects involving sensor networks, mobile technologies and science education. Before coming to RCA, he served as Course Director of the MRes in Information Environments at London College of Communication, University of the Arts London, and worked as an artist.

    Suzy Hogg

    After studying Graphic Design, Suzy Hogg began her early career as an editorial designer, working as an Art Director on a breadth of newsstand magazine and newspaper titles. Arriving at The Guardian in the mid-90’s, she was asked to work on the UK launch of Wired magazine. Once introduced to the excitement surrounding the ‘dawn of the digital revolution’ in San Francisco at the time, there was no looking back and her journey into digital design began. From free and pioneering online explorations during the 90’s, through to now more thoughtful, human-centred, creative problem-solving approaches to designing digital experiences, she now works on audience-led design research, designs digital service and brand experiences and delivers design strategies, for clients ranging from world-class media organisations, to national institutions and treasure houses.   During a recent study sabbatical, she began consulting at The British Museum, supporting the Head of Digital to craft a strategic framework to inform it’s future digital services online and off-line in galleries. This immersion in a museum environment has inspired her to move towards the field of interaction design for physical spaces and further research into visitor identity and behavior, with a particular emphasis on how curiosity when piqued can intensify an experience, resulting in interesting learning outcomes.

    David di Duca

    David Di Duca first started studying architecture at UCL in 2002 and has since acquired a wide range of both professional and academic experience in architecture and interactive design. Amongst a variety of current projects and interests, David is working for University College London as a research assistant on Design with Heritage, a year long AHRC funded research project being co-run by UCL and the Victoria and Albert Museum. The focus of the project is to explore new ways in which technology can be utilised to enhance visitor experiences within heritage institutions. His research work investigates ideas relating to affordance, human-nonhuman interaction and cybernetics. This research is developed through building interactive test installations. These projects utilise computer vision and machine learning to explore relationships between people and the objects they encounter. For his doctorate he is supported by Jason Bruges Studio and is supervised by Professor Stephen Gage and Dr Sean Hanna.
