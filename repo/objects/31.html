The Past is a Social Network: New Directions in Public Heritage and Collective Memory in a Digital Age
Date added:  2013-09-17 20:47:15
Modified:        2013-09-19 12:52:46
Author / Creator Neil A. Silberman
In the span of just forty years — from the final drafting and ratification of the UNESCO World Heritage Convention in 1972 to the present — our understanding of the role of heritage in society has undergone a series of far-reaching changes. The core concepts of ‘scientific accuracy’, ‘authenticity’, ‘outstanding universal value’, and even ‘monument’ are now the subjects of bitter debate. Among many professionals, the term ‘conservation’ has given way to ‘safeguarding’. Intangible Heritage of beliefs, customs, and craftsmanship — alongside architecture and archaeology — has become a major official concern. The goal of encouraging ‘Cultural Diversity’ has shouldered aside linear aesthetic trajectories. The development of heritage tourist attractions for economic reasons, no less than historical commemoration, is a driving force in the field. This paper will analyze how Digital Heritage has exerted a major impact in all of these changes, and will survey some achievements, failures and potentials for the further transformation of cultural heritage in the 21st Century. Foremost among these potentials, this paper will argue, is the ability of Digital Heritage to facilitate simultaneous top-down and bottom-up public reflection and communication on heritage significance, its contemporary relevance and its relation to personal and group identity. The paper will explain how digitally-based, worldwide ‘memory communities’ may soon become as important to the safeguarding of cultural heritage sites as physical tourism or the shrinking capacities of national monuments authorities. In a world of diasporic movement, cultural hybridity and the use and abuse of heritage icons as potent political symbols, public heritage presentation is already shifting from officially authorized narratives to loud, often passionate, public discussion. Whether that discussion will become increasingly destructive, delusional or democratic will depend in large measure on the emerging character of digital heritage tools.
