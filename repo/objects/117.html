Lean Museum Startup: A low budget – low risk approach to transforming cultural institutions
Date added:  2013-10-15 14:09:30
Modified:        2013-10-15 14:09:30
Author / Creator Karsten Greschhttp://www.softwareag.com
Author / Creator Martin Hegel
In 2011, the IT engineer Eric Ries published a book called “Lean Startup”. It was about how people from startups (entrepreneurs) as well as of large enterprises (intrapreneurs) could establish business models effectively: with low risks and with low budget. The technique behind this new methodology is understood as validated learning. The aim of a startup is not making cash, but finding a sustainable and even disruptive business model. Validation implies especially “pivoting”: If you see that a hypothesis cannot be validated, you change your direction and try another hypothesis. Ries himself refers very often to disruptive innovations and why they are absolutely critical for companies to survive. Why should knowing about disruptive innovations and commercial enterprises be relevant for cultural institutions? If we understand content as the main value any cultural institution can offer, we’re currently facing many disruptive innovations that influence museums and other institutions now directly: Content is spread globally in no time via the internet. People communicate globally on social networks. More and more people see the world filtered through smartphones and other devices. They expect content differently, want to interact with it, want to be educated as well as entertained. Any cultural institution has to proof to a global community why its content is worth being recognised. As the audience is global, offerings could need to be diversified to the extreme. Many cultural institutions have not only a lack of budget and staff, but also barriers between internal units and problems in transforming the museum. Applying Lean Startup methodology to museums needs to be understandable for non-business people. Lean Museum Startup (LMS) shows how internal projects can be understood as intrapreneurial startups and how to bring people together and start validating ideas and innovating the museum. The poster shows how to apply LMS in practice.

    Karsten Gresch

    Karsten Gresch (1968) is a lawyer and computer engineer. He earned his assessor iuris (law master equivalent) at the Freie Universität Berlin, worked as an advocate and finally switched to the IT business where he worked for different companies (amongst them Skandia) in various technical positions. At his current employer, Software AG, he is the technical responsible for the cloud media platform, a research project offering a semantic search engine for art exhibits, books and more (http://is.gd/cloudmediasag). Karsten also founded the Lean Museum Startup initiative (http://leanmusemstartup.com) and has special interests in entrepreneurship, business model generation and all facets of innovation and interdisciplinarity. Thanks to becoming well-bred through family and education, he loves the arts, e.g. Venetian painting from Renaissance to Tiepolo, contemporary arts, poetry, classical music (both historic and contemporary) and the many interfaces between arts and IT. Karsten is married and father of five (2+3) children.

    Martin Hegel

    After studying business management and communication science Martin started his working career in advertising back in the 1990’s at the Frankfurt office of the global agency network Saatchi & Saatchi. He gained indepth experience of FMCG and retail and worked for brands such as Danone, Fuji and Samsung. He left in 2002 as a member of the management board having been in charge of several national and international clients. After a period at the Berlin office of the TBWA agency network, where he took responsibility for campaigns for Apple and Absolut Vodka, he has been focussing on communication and strategic tasks in the field of art and museum marketing until today. After creating numerous value added exhibition campaigns for several cultural institutions Martin was then especially successful in re-launching the brand identity and communication concept of the 200-year-old Städel Museum in 2010/11, one of the most important art collections in Germany. Parallel to the brand developing process he refined all its communication channels and products that increase the interaction between the museum and its visitors, such as advertising, social media, analogue and digital guides, apps, merchandising, the collections’ catalogues and various educational programs. The over all target of the repositioning was to develop a cultural brand experience which overcame the barriers between the physical visit and virtual contact with the collection while strengthening the potential of the individual experience and information needs of each visitor. Today Martin is working as the Head of Communication Design at the Museum of Applied Arts in Frankfurt translating the museum’s strategy “space for possibilities” into all its main disciplines, including exhibitions, education and communication, across analogue and digital channels. Moreover he also does strategic consultancy for the digital design agency Orange Hive and the Lean Museum Startup initiative.
