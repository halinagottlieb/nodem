Houses Built on Sand: Ensuring Secure Foundations for the Preservation of and Access to Digital Heritage
Date added:  2013-09-17 16:33:25
Modified:        2014-09-11 16:17:17
Author / Creator Susan Whitfield
While we are all in the business of interpreting our heritage to reach as wide an audience as possible, we are increasingly driven to this by the need to attract and satisfy funders. Yet we have to balance the use of scarce resources on preparing and maintaining digital heritage with our responsibilities for the preservation and access to the physical heritage. How do we ensure we exploit new technologies to showcase our heritage and attract a new audience, while continuing our core responsibilities — and reconcile both these with increasingly challenged budgets? At its foundation in 1994, The International Dunhuang Project (IDP) made a commitment to exploit fully the emerging technologies of the internet and digitization to ensure continued preservation and greater access to the archaeological legacy of Chinese Central Asia. As a largely externally-funded endeavour, it has always had to have one eye on attracting potential funders and increasing user figures. And as a collaboration of largely public institutions, it also has a commitment to long-term preservation both of the original collections and the digital resources. This presentation will offer IDP as a case study in the adoption of ‘a middle way’ to achieve these aims. Creating data in itself is a largely unlauded labour but, like architectural foundations, the resulting data has the potential to support edifices that can dazzle and entice in both this and generations to come. The presentation will also showcase IDP’s current work on designing a relatively simple system to record standard and multilingual metadata for multimedia heritage — from a manuscript to an archaeological site — enabling them to be managed, searched and accessed on a unified interface. The model will be made freely available, part of IDP’s continuing commitment to share its experience and expertise.

Susan Whitfield

International Dunhuang Project British Library. Dr. Susan Whitfield is an historian of medieval China and the Silk Road and curator of the Stein and related collections of 50,000 Central Asian manuscripts from Dunhuang and other Silk Road sites at the British Library. She has curated several major exhibitions, lectures internationally and has published many books and articles. She also travels regularly along the Silk Road. Using technology, old and new, to ensure that the collections are preserved for future generations while made fully accessible for this generation has always been an essential part of her work at the British Library. In 1994, along with colleagues worldwide, she was instrumental in establishing an international collaboration to achieve these aims. Although named ‘The International Dunhuang Project’ (IDP) its remit includes all archaeological artefacts from sites throughout Chinese Central Asia. With ten major partners hosting multilingual websites in IDP Centres worldwide and over thirty other collaborating institutions, IDP now gives access to hundreds of thousands of images of the Silk Road artefacts and manuscripts, along with rich contextual information. Most recently, Dr. Whitfield has started work with British Library colleagues on developing projects with institutions and scholars in Afghanistan. She is currently working on several articles and a book, as well as a complete redesign of the IDP database, all due in 2013.
