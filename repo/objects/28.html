Methods, Meaning and Uses of Life Size Resolution Imagery of Tibetan Murals
Date added:  2013-09-17 20:31:11
Modified:        2013-09-19 12:53:17
Author / Creator Thomas Laird
My first images of the murals of Tibet, shot forty years ago, failed to convey their power, and it’s the lack of good images of the murals of Tibet that have kept them largely unknown. My work is motivated by a desire to change that, to bring this art to the world. Starting ten years ago I began to create ‘life size resolution images’ of giant murals: today more than 300 murals have been captured, some as large as 10 x 30 feet. The use of digital capture and render tools allows me to make images that create the illusion that one is standing in front of the original. When approaching hyper-realistic images of ancient Tibetan murals, different people see different meanings. Curators at institutions have been overheard to say, ‘We exhibit art, not images of art’ even though without these new digital tools such art would remain unknown, even as it is threatened with disappearing. A monk standing in front of the same image looks once at the mural and then launches into a dialogue about the Buddhist meanings depicted in the mural. He begins curtly, ‘Now, the meaning’. The image of the mural allows a conversation with him, or with anyone who knows Tibetan art or history, that would otherwise be impossible. Such images allow art historians to study a thousand years of Tibetan murals. They could be used by conservators. They are being used by scholars to translate inscriptions on the murals. They allow contemporary Tibetan artists to copy designs for modern works. They are the only means by which museum visitors can see the greatest surviving art treasures of Tibet. Yet these same images allow a monk to give a precise illustrated lecture to students about a particular meditation practice. Simultaneously,for those who know nothing about Tibet, an entire chapter of humanities’ art heritage is made visible for the first time. When institutions try to decide who should assess my work I hear them thinking to themselves: Should this be assessed by the Photography Curator, the Asian Art Curator or the Modern Art Curator? In my mind the answer is one word. Yes. In the 21st century such digital art leaps entirely over the issue of provenance that plagues and empowers so many ‘original works of Asian antique art’ – even as it allows unprecedented correlations to be established between historical schools of art, and art trends, visible in the portable pieces within a museum, as seen through the lens of large scale murals. The digital capture of mankind’s heritage will go forward, at an accelerated pace, with ever greater resolution, in striking new ways that invariably raise questions about appropriation, authenticity, and the nature of originality. Just as photography itself did a century ago. We do not need to know the answers to these questions to know that the task assigned to those of us, who now have tools that allow this, is both urgent and monumental. Future generations will ask ‘You mean you were able to capture this art, and you let it disappear without any record?’ Their awe, at every endangered work of art that we can see, that we can share, is the only reason we need to understand as we embark on this work.

    Thomas Laird

    Independent Photographer. Thomas C. Laird is an American photographer and writer who has explored the art, culture, history and modern life of Himalayan people for the past 40 years. He was based in Nepal for decades as a journalist, contributing to Time, Newsweek and dozens of other magazines. His book of photographs of the murals of the Lukhang, 18th century murals in the Dalai Lama’s private meditation chamber in Lhasa, was first published by Thames and Hudson in 2000 as The Dalai Lama’s Secret Temple. His history of Tibet The Story of Tibet: Conversations with the Dalai Lama, Grove, 2006, is now available in 16 languages. He has created the largest photo library in the world on Tibetan and Himalayan murals, the first such archive in life size resolution. His most recent work, an exhibition of life size resolution prints of ten murals in the Lukhang, is on permanent exhibition at The Rubin Museum in New York City. He is currently working on Murals of Tibet, to be published by TASCHEN in two editions: in a Double Elephant Folio, as last used by Audubon, Birds of America, 1853, and in a standard-sized edition. He divides his time between Asia and New Orleans.
