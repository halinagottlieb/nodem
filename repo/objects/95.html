Europeana
Date added:  2013-10-06 09:56:39
Modified:        2013-10-18 12:29:05
Author / Creator Nanna Thylstrup
One of EU’s biggest cultural prestige projects is the Internet portal Europeana, a site that gives access to digitized European cultural heritage. At first sight the portal appears to be little more than a primitive Internet site. But in reality the portal makes for one of the most interesting studies of the economic and political potential of cultural heritage in the digital sphere. I will give insight into Europeana as a conceptual, political and economic project. Hereafter I will highlight some important differences between state-funded and privately funded digitization: 1) terms of access 2) selection criteria (‘Googlearchy’ vs ‘Enlightenment ideology’) 3) the role of the citizen in the formation the institutional archive EUROPEANA Cultural institutions and archives used to be perceived as selective realms contributing to - and serving the needs of - specialists and people with a particular interest in the institution’s possessions. But digitization and the Internet has fundamentally refuelled the importance and redefined the notion of the cultural archive, making them an integral part of ordinary citizens’ daily lives, both as users and contributors. The key factor in this dramatic shift has been the invention of search engines and digital databases turning physically confined specialist sites into digitally omnipresent layman sites. And the change has generated new scholarly, political and corporate interest in the formation and governance of archives. The archives of cultural heritage thus embody the issues arising from what American professor of Law, Madhavi Sunder, has named the recent rise of IP3: the unification of identity politics, the Internet Protocol, and intellectual property. This transformation can be seen in the case of Europeana. For almost a decade, the European Commission has been working on the digitization of, and online accessibility to, European cultural heritage. The process started with co-funded projects meant to facilitate cooperation between Member States and individual arts institutions and their efforts to digitize parts of their cultural heritage. It was, however, not until June 2005 that the project accelerated as a result of the Commission’s decision to make the European digital archive, Europeana, one of the flagship projects of the EU’s i2010 strategy. Subsequently Europeana has raised a long range of central political, conceptual and institutional issues concerning the digitization of cultural heritage – issues that address everyone working with the Internet, from individual citizens to institutions, corporations and politicians.
