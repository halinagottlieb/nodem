Working in beta – implications for museums and their visitors
Date added:  2013-10-16 12:39:07
Modified:        2014-01-15 19:08:39
Author / Creator Lauren Davishttp://www.ku.edu.tr/en
Author / Creator Christie Rayhttp://www.uva.nl/en/disciplin...
Author / Creator Merel van der Vaarthttp://www.uva.nl/over-de-uva/...
As the inclusion of New Media is fast becoming an established component of the exhibition development process, a stark division is becoming apparent between a small number of big, often national, museums with the capacity to maintain in-house digital media departments, and the vast majority of museums which must outsource digital technology projects. The University of Amsterdam’s Allard Pierson Museum is exploring new, low-budget ways in which smaller museums can develop digital installations in-house. The museum’s collaborative research programs and technical partners from European universities have created opportunities to test and evaluate developing technologies. This paper covers two such technologies: an Augmented Reality (AR) iPad application and Leap Motion technology. Both were implemented in a new Ancient Egypt exhibition in July 2013. The AR software gives museums unprecedented access to content creation and implementation without requiring specialized knowledge or outsourcing by providing an internet-based template. Sensor-based Leap Motion technology was used by the Allard Pierson Museum as a low-cost tool for offering visitors digital interactions with physical objects. In both cases, the museum is working with beta versions of the software and remains in constant dialogue with the developers to fix problems and deliver feedback, also after the installations were released to the public. This paper compares the overall effectiveness of both technologies, examining the benefits and consequences of working with beta technology for smaller museums. In order to better understand the impact of this way of working, we identified two areas of research. First, we evaluated the ease of use of the two technologies for the museum itself, including limitations, and the potential for other museums and future use. Second, we analyzed visitor responses to and experiences with the new technologies. Our observations were supplemented with visitor interviews and evaluation forms in order to obtain quantifiable feedback.

    Lauren Davis

    Lauren Davis is a Ph.D. student at Koç University in Istanbul, Turkey and currently interns at the Smithsonian Institute’s Freer|Sackler Galleries in Washington, D.C. Her background is in archaeology, and she has participated in archaeological excavations in America, Turkey, and Israel. In addition to the Smithsonian, she has worked with several museums and heritage centers, including the University of Pennsylvania Museum, the Penn Cultural Heritage Center, and the Allard Pierson Museum in Amsterdam. Among her research interests are museums, cultural heritage, memory, and digitization. Starting with research done at the summer 2012 “Urban Cultural Heritage and Creative Practices” workshop in Istanbul, she began exploring Istanbul’s intangible heritage and history through smellscapes. Her dissertation research focuses on bringing the sensory experience, and smell in particular, into our understanding of place and heritage, and how the sensory landscapes and heritage can be integrated with the digital world. As part of her dissertation work, she will also be turning her smellscape research into an exhibition.

    Christie Ray

    Christie A. Ray is a PhD researcher with the V-MusT.net project at the University of Amsterdam, focusing on evaluating the user experience of New Media applications within the museum context. As part of her work with V-MusT.net, she helped establish the NewMediaLab, integrated into the permanent exhibition space of Allard Pierson Museum, to serve as a location where New Media applications could be tested and evaluated with real museum visitors in the museum environment. She participated in the Etruscanning project with the development of The Virtual Reconstruction of the Regolini-Galassi Tomb, assisting with archaeological research, storytelling and evaluation of the installation with museum users. She has previously earned Master’s degrees in Roman Archaeology and Museology, which motivated her to explore the use of technology as an interpretive tool for archaeological collections. Her research interests include visitor interactivity with technology inside of museums, levels of accessibility to collections content, and improving the visitor’s museum experience through integrated New Media installations. The aim of her dissertation will be to demonstrate the unique nature of the museum context and to show how the museum experience for visitors can be affected by the integration of New Media applications into the museum context.

    Merel van der Vaart

    Merel van der Vaart is a PhD student at the University of Amsterdam, the Netherlands. She is based at the Allard Pierson Museum, the University’s archaeology museum. Her research focusses on the (changing) role of the physical museum object as part of ‘technology-heavy’ museum displays. So far, her research has explored the use of Augmented Reality and virtually restored object replicas. She previously worked at the Science Museum in London as its first Associate Curator of Public History, where she was responsible for co-creative projects both online and on-site. In 2010 she was Research Assistant for the LIVE!Museum project at the University of Leicester’s School of Museum Studies. She wrote her master thesis on the way on-site digital installations impact on social interaction among visitors. Special interests include: the relationship between people, objects and information; audience engagement, participation & co-creation; social interaction in the museum space; and merging online and on-site experiences. Merel holds degrees in Museology (cum laude) and Language & Culture Studies , as well as an MA in Museum Studies.
