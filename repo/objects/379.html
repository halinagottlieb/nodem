DOMUS: An On-Gallery Digital Museum Experience in Two Parts
Date added:  2014-11-24 21:24:20
Modified:        2015-01-22 17:24:53
Author / Creator Merel van der Vaarthttp://www.uva.nl/over-de-uva/...
Author / Creator Christie Rayhttp://www.uva.nl/en/disciplin...
The Allard Pierson Museum, the archaeology museum of the University of Amsterdam, will be redeveloping its Roman gallery. As part of this redevelopment, it is exploring how virtual re-contextualization and interactive technologies could be used to aid visitor interpretation of its collections. Based on earlier studies on the use of gesture-based navigation in virtual (museum) environments, the Museum developed and tested an interactive prototype consisting of two parts. In the first part, the visitor uses gesture-based interaction to navigate through a Roman house and collect seven virtual replicas of museum objects. In the second part, the visitor can discover the real objects nearby in a display case and explore additional information using an associated touchscreen. The prototype was installed in the Museum in order to study both the effect of virtual contextualization, and the learnability of this type of gesture- based navigation. Asking visitors to operate new technologies to interact with virtual objects and environments naturally poses some usability issues. Through a series of observations and interviews with adult visitors, the Museum has examined the impact of instruction on the use of this type of interactive. The study compared the ability for visitors to navigate the virtual space after receiving one of two forms of instruction and asked them about their instruction needs and ease of use of the installation. Furthermore, the Museum wanted to better understand how visitors see the relationship between both the virtual installation and the real objects. Through interviews and guided visits, we examined whether the use of virtual replicas and environments in the museum serve to support the interpretation of the physical collections. This paper will discuss the development of the installation, the research outcomes, and will reflect upon potential future developments that might improve the installation for inclusion in the Museum’s new Roman gallery.

Merel van der Vaart

Merel van der Vaart is a PhD student at the University of Amsterdam, the Netherlands. She is based at the Allard Pierson Museum, the University’s archaeology museum. Her research focusses on the (changing) role of the physical museum object as part of ‘technology-heavy’ museum displays. So far, her research has explored the use of Augmented Reality and virtually restored object replicas. She previously worked at the Science Museum in London as its first Associate Curator of Public History, where she was responsible for co-creative projects both online and on-site. In 2010 she was Research Assistant for the LIVE!Museum project at the University of Leicester’s School of Museum Studies. She wrote her master thesis on the way on-site digital installations impact on social interaction among visitors. Special interests include: the relationship between people, objects and information; audience engagement, participation & co-creation; social interaction in the museum space; and merging online and on-site experiences. Merel holds degrees in Museology (cum laude) and Language & Culture Studies , as well as an MA in Museum Studies.

Christie Ray

Christie A. Ray is a PhD researcher with the V-MusT.net project at the University of Amsterdam, focusing on evaluating the user experience of New Media applications within the museum context. As part of her work with V-MusT.net, she helped establish the NewMediaLab, integrated into the permanent exhibition space of Allard Pierson Museum, to serve as a location where New Media applications could be tested and evaluated with real museum visitors in the museum environment. She participated in the Etruscanning project with the development of The Virtual Reconstruction of the Regolini-Galassi Tomb, assisting with archaeological research, storytelling and evaluation of the installation with museum users. She has previously earned Master’s degrees in Roman Archaeology and Museology, which motivated her to explore the use of technology as an interpretive tool for archaeological collections. Her research interests include visitor interactivity with technology inside of museums, levels of accessibility to collections content, and improving the visitor’s museum experience through integrated New Media installations. The aim of her dissertation will be to demonstrate the unique nature of the museum context and to show how the museum experience for visitors can be affected by the integration of New Media applications into the museum context.
